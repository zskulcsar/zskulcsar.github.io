A few days ago I was having a chat with a junior(ish) engineer and we touched on AI assisted coding. At some point she said that **"[agents] are really good at prototypes!"**. Which of course I agreed with, but then in the comfort of my home I started to think about this statement a little.

## Agents are really good at prototypes ... but why?

I should say, the answer might surprise you, but this is not youtube, instagram or tiktok. I think the answer comes down to our perception of a prototype and its intended purpose. A prototype, by definition, is a shot in the dark to see what we can hit? Or perhaps to see if we can hit what we're aiming at? Typically we can vocalise what we want in rough terms, then the agent goes away, does its things and produces something that gives us ideas, confirms our thinking, or invalidates our thinking for that matter.

The main point here is that we gauge the result with extremely lenient eyes. A prototype doesn't need to be perfect, it just needs to show that the direction is right, or not. The prototype's quality isn't the point; what matters is whether we can see the future direction in it.

Also, a prototype is typically something that is destined to prove an idea. i.e., when we're creating said prototype we're looking for the quick win, the positive feedback. I don't think I've ever seen a prototype that meant to prove: this can't be done, but how can it be done? It's our endorphin hit confirming we're right.

Now, there is no revelation here, but I think it is important to understand the difference between a prototype and the final product and be conscious about it.

On the prototype we have an input box for the local currency amount and a dropdown for the target currency. We select, the JavaScript recalculates, it's working. Great, we have our dopamine hit.
Whereas in the final product we need to fetch the exchange rate, we might want to calculate commissions, we might want to persist the fetched exchange rate, we might want to have default if the exchange rate can't be pulled, we might want to have audit records, we might need some observability, we might need to deal with other failure modes. The list is endless. **Endless**. It takes time, we need to think about it, it's slow. Questions upon questions and we just can't see the final product. Small wins matter, but we don't get the same dopamine hit from small wins.

The prototype is an "illustrative example" while the product is the final calculation with real numbers, real rules and the paperwork attached.

What I'm saying here is that the prototype, the one-shot example, posted on LinkedIn is just an illusion. Smoke and mirrors, yet people fall into the trap, forgetting how much effort and dedication need to go into any product from prototype to final product. We invented all sorts of ways to shorten that path and IMHO, AI is just another tool to shorten that path. 


The effort with AI is less, but that doesn't mean it can be skipped. In fact, using AI for bridging the gap can slow things down. Sure, code generation is quicker, but then AI asks all these questions: but what about the exchange rate? what about persistence? what about audit? what about observability? what about failure modes?
Or even worse: it does not and just goes away and generates the code based on assumptions we are not even aware of. Then we end up with a smorgasbord of a quagmire where the same thing is done in different ways over and over again. Where there are no rules, there are as many solutions as one can imagine. Anything goes, as long as it was in the training data.

This is, of course, no different from human development teams. But as it is slower and humans talk with each other, there is a built in quality control. We check the code, we call out the silliness, we point out that this has been completed already, we do a bit of planning and our laziness gets the better of us, so we reuse. The resulting software will have a level of inherent consistency that scales with the understanding of the team, and sadly ends there.

My feeling is that by focusing on speed of delivery, we're missing the important opportunity to create better software. In the past 50 years the main constraint preventing us from writing better software was time. We needed to deliver X, but we didn't have enough developers (there are never enough developers) so we cut corners. As a side effect, this also exerts pressure and drives us towards reuse and some sort of internal consistency. Right now, we are given more developers (in the form of multiple agents instructed by one person) yet we're focusing even more on how quickly we can deliver. We instruct, we deliver, no code-review, does it work? yes, ship and we hope that the agents' code will be consistent. Which it is to an extent, but then those small differences between sessions are creeping up as the results of the matrix multiplications slowly shift, triggering a different set of probabilities.

Inevitably, this results in a mess. The quick and dirty phase is quick and really dirty, but then reality hits and we need to spend time clearing the mess - time we must spend elsewhere - because we can generate more mess so quickly. And frankly, just because we have more agents, it doesn't mean that they can produce better software quicker. If I have a 100x mess then I need 100x more agents to clear that mess. Is that a speed-up?

I keep hearing people say "It's fine, we just ask the agent to rewrite". But then we've spent time and money to create the first quick and dirty version in order to rewrite later. This honestly boils my blood, especially when this is a flippant reply by senior engineers. As if they had forgotten the past 50 years and the safeguards we invented.

That being said, there are cases when a prototype is good enough. There is space for that, there is need for that but atm it seems folks are blinded by Prototype Driven Development (PDD) and its speed delivering software with abysmal quality.

Don't do that folks, resist the urge and do your job. In the PDD world the real differentiator will be quality.
